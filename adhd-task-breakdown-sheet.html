ADHD task breakdown — the four-column method that gets tasks started

Last updated: November 12, 2026

"Just break it into smaller tasks" is the advice ADHD brains hear most and use least — because nobody shows what "smaller" means. A task like "do taxes" broken into "gather documents, fill forms, file" is still three doors with no handles. The four-column method below defines small as something you can see yourself doing in the next five minutes. That bar is the whole trick.

Why "break it down" fails ADHD brains

Executive function is the brain's project manager — the part that starts things, holds steps in mind, and switches between them. When a task's first move is vague ("start the report"), starting requires the manager to invent a plan from nothing, and ADHD brains stall exactly there. The fix isn't motivation; it's making the first move so concrete that starting needs no planning at all — just doing the visible thing in front of you.

The four columns

One row per task, four columns:

  1. The task — written the way it lives in your head ("do taxes").
  2. Smallest visible first move — physical, observable, ≤5 minutes: "open the tax folder and put last year's return on the desk." Not "gather documents." Visible means someone watching could tell you did it.
  3. What "started" looks like — the state that proves you began: "folder open, return on desk, pen out." This kills the ambiguity that lets a started task feel unfinished.
  4. What happens after 5 minutes — the pre-decided fork: "keep going if it's flowing; if not, close the folder and that still counts as a win." The permission to stop is what makes starting cheap.

The 5-minute rule, defined properly

The version that works has the fourth column. "Just do it for five minutes" fails without a defined stop, because an open-ended five minutes feels like a commitment to the whole task. With the fork pre-decided, starting costs five minutes and carries no obligation — which is why it works on days when nothing else does.

FAQ

How do I break down tasks with ADHD?

Make the first move visible: write the task, then define a physical first step of five minutes or less — something an observer could see you do. Then note what "started" looks like and what you'll do after five minutes. Vague steps stall ADHD brains; visible, small, pre-forked steps don't need motivation to begin.

What is the 5-minute rule for ADHD?

A starting tactic: commit only to five minutes of a task you're avoiding, with the stop pre-decided — after five minutes you're free to continue or quit, and quitting still counts. The pre-decided exit is what makes it work; open-ended starts feel like commitments to the whole task and get avoided.

Why do I procrastinate on tasks I know how to do?

Because knowing how isn't the barrier — starting is. ADHD procrastination is usually a first-move problem: the task's entry point is undefined, so beginning demands planning energy the brain won't spend. Defining a visible five-minute first move moves the effort from deciding to doing.

Is task breakdown the same as a to-do list?

No. A to-do list stores tasks; a breakdown converts one stuck task into a startable first move plus a definition of "started" plus an exit plan. Lists fail ADHD brains at the starting line; breakdowns are built for exactly that moment.
