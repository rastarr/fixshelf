DTF print settings — the pre-run checklist that saves a film run

Last updated: November 12, 2026

The prints that waste film are almost never random — they trace back to a check nobody ran: the file that wasn't mirrored, the ink density that was set for the last film batch, the powder laid on wet ink. Here's the checklist a shop tapes to the printer. Every number below is a starting point to verify against your own film and powder spec sheets — your consumables override anything on the internet, including this page.

File checks before you print

  • Mirror the art. DTF prints face-down onto film; unmirrored text presses on backwards. Check by flipping any text layer before export.
  • Transparency where it belongs, only where it belongs. White at 0% opacity disappears on press and the garment shows through — knock out backgrounds, but keep intended whites as solid white ink channels.
  • Resolution at print size. Art that looks sharp on screen but is a 40% scale-up in the RIP prints soft. Check the final placed size, not the source file.
  • Gang-up placement. Designs too close together let the powder bridge between transfers; leave a clean gutter.

Printer settings that matter

Ink density — the setting people ask about most — is how much ink the head lays down per pass. Too low and colors wash out and whites turn translucent after pressing; too high and ink pools, drying slows, and the powder sticks where it shouldn't. It's the dial that moves when you change film batches or notice whites failing. Alongside it: print resolution per your RIP's mode, the correct color mode for your workflow, and a daily nozzle/head check strip — banding you catch on a test strip is free; banding you catch on a press is a shirt.

Powder and cure checks

Powder goes on evenly, while the ink is wet — uneven coverage reads as patchy adhesion after the first wash. The melt stage matters as much: the powder needs to flow to a smooth gel before curing, not just warm up. Cure temperature and time follow your film and powder spec sheet, and they move when you change consumable brands — write the working numbers on the machine, not in someone's memory.

Heat press starting points

For common DTF film and powder systems, shops typically start around 290–320°F (roughly 145–160°C) for 10–15 seconds at medium-firm pressure, with a second 5-second post-press after peeling depending on film type. These are starting points only: run a test press on scrap, check peel and wash behavior, and let your consumables' spec sheet set the final number.

The 60-second preflight routine

  1. Flip a text layer — mirrored? ✓
  2. Check whites at print size — solid, sharp? ✓
  3. Test strip — no banding? ✓
  4. Ink density set for this film batch? ✓
  5. Powder coverage even, ink wet? ✓
  6. Cure numbers match the current spec sheet? ✓
  7. Press temp/time/pressure set and test-pressed? ✓

Thirty seconds per run buys back the film, the shirt and the customer argument. FAQ

What does ink density mean in DTF printing?

Ink density is how much ink the printer lays down per pass. Too little and prints come out washed-out with translucent whites; too much and ink pools, drying drags, and powder adheres where it shouldn't. It's the first setting to check when whites fail or colors look thin after pressing.

What temperature do you press DTF transfers?

Typical starting range: 290–320°F (145–160°C) for 10–15 seconds at medium-firm pressure, often with a short second press after peeling. Treat that as a starting point — your film and powder spec sheet overrides any number on the internet, so test-press and verify wash results before production.

Why are my DTF prints banding?

Most often: clogged nozzles or a missed head check, ink density set too low for the current film, or art scaled beyond its resolution at print size. Run a test strip first — banding caught on a strip costs seconds; caught on a pressed shirt it costs the garment.

Do you mirror DTF prints?

Yes — DTF prints face-down onto film, so the art must be mirrored before printing or it presses onto the garment backwards. The quick check: flip any text layer and see if it reads correctly reversed on the film's print side.
