Vegetable garden planner printable — map the beds, time the plantings, log the season

Last updated: November 12, 2026

Most garden "planning" is a planting list — and planting lists fail by July, because a list doesn't say what goes where, when it goes in, or what actually produced last year. A garden plan that gets used has three parts: a map, a calendar, and a log. Here's how each one works on paper.

The three parts of a garden plan that gets used

A list tells you what to buy. A map tells you what goes where — and prevents planting the shade-lover behind the six-foot tomatoes. A calendar tells you when, anchored to your frost dates. The log is the part everyone skips and the part that compounds: it's the reason year three is better than year one. Lists alone fail because they answer none of the three questions that actually decide a season.

Mapping your beds

Draw each bed to scale on a gridded page — graph paper is fine. Three passes:

  1. Sun — note which edges get blasted by afternoon light and which stay shaded; this decides the layout more than anything else.
  2. Spacing — place crops by their real spacing (the seed packet's, not the wishful one), tall crops on the north edge so they don't shade everything else.
  3. Paths — mark where you'll actually walk. A bed you can't reach into without stepping in it is a bed you'll stop weeding by June.

The planting calendar

Everything keys off one number you write at the top of the page: your last frost date. Frost dates are local — look yours up for your own ZIP code and write it on the calendar page. Then work backwards: tomatoes started indoors about 6–8 weeks before it, peas and greens sown well before it, heat-lovers like basil long after. The calendar's job is turning "when do I plant X?" into arithmetic you only do once.

What to log during the season

Four lines per crop is a complete log: what you planted and when, what produced (and roughly how much, so "the tomatoes did great" becomes "Roma outgrew Cherry"), what got eaten or diseased, and one honest verdict — plant again, change something, or skip next year. The log is the page that makes next year's map and calendar better instead of starting over.

FAQ

How do I plan a vegetable garden layout?

Measure your beds, note how many hours of sun each spot gets, sketch the beds to scale, place tall crops on the north edge, group plants by family so rotation is easy next year, and mark the paths before you place anything. One gridded page per bed keeps the whole layout visible at a glance.

When should I start vegetables indoors?

As a rule of thumb, most starts go 6–8 weeks before your last frost date — tomatoes and peppers at the front of that window, lettuce and brassicas closer to it. Your own frost date decides the actual week: look it up locally and count backwards on the calendar.

What is crop rotation and do I need it?

Rotation means not planting the same plant family in the same spot year after year — it starves the pests and soil problems that specialize in that family. The simplest rule: four groups (fruiting, roots, leafy, legumes) and each bed moves one step along that order each year. Small gardens benefit too.

Is a paper or app garden planner better?

By criteria: apps win reminders and automatic frost dates; paper wins visibility and durability — the map hangs where you garden, takes pencil notes in the dirt-era moments, and never needs a battery. Many gardeners run paper for the plan and log, and their phone for timer-level reminders.
