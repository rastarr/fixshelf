The salary negotiation script: word-for-word, both directions

Last updated: November 12, 2026

Most salary advice is about confidence. But on the actual call — offer on the table, a pause getting longer — confidence doesn't say the sentence. A script does. Below are the sentences for the three moments that decide money: the ask, the email version, and the pushback. Adapt the bracketed fills; keep the structure.

The one rule before any script

Get the number first — and avoid naming the first number when you can. Whoever names a number first anchors the range, and every counter happens inside that anchor. Common practice, not law: sometimes the recruiter simply won't move without one. If you're forced to name a number, name a researched range whose bottom is a salary you'd actually accept — never a single bare figure, and never a range you'd turn down.

The offer-call script

When the number lands, don't respond to it live. The pause is the whole move:

  1. Thank them genuinely: "Thank you — I'm really pleased about this offer, and I'm excited about the role."
  2. Then the sentence that buys you a day: "Could I take a day or two to review the full package? I'd like to give it proper consideration." Almost nobody says no to this, and it removes every pressure to negotiate cold.
  3. When you call back, the ask itself: "I'm excited about the team and the work. Based on my research for this role and market in [city], and what I bring — [your two or three strongest wins] — I was expecting a base salary closer to [fill]. Is there flexibility?"

Then stop talking. The silence after "Is there flexibility?" is where raises live. The first person to fill it usually concedes something.

The email version

If the offer arrived in writing, reply in writing — it's calmer and it creates a record:

"Hi [name], thank you again for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about joining [team]. Before I accept, I'd like to discuss the base salary. Based on my research for this role in [location] and my experience in [your strongest relevant win], I was expecting a base in the range of [fill]. Is there flexibility on the base? Happy to jump on a call — I can be flexible on timing. Thanks again for the offer, and for considering this."

Notice what the email does: enthusiasm stated before the ask, one reason (research + one win, not five), one number-range question, and gratitude at the end. Nothing else belongs in it.

The pushback replies

This is the section most guides skip, and it's where negotiations actually end:

"The budget for this role is fixed." — "I understand budgets are set. If the base can't move, is there flexibility on [signing bonus / an earlier review at six months / additional leave / remote days]?" You accept their frame and move to a lever they control.

"We'll revisit your salary in six months." — "I appreciate that. Could we put the review in writing, with the criteria we'd both look at? That way we're working from the same page in six months." A vague promise converts to a written criterion, or it reveals itself as a brush-off.

"Another candidate was stronger / we have someone cheaper." — "I understand. Given you've chosen to make me this offer, I assume you believe I'll deliver — my ask is based on the value of that delivery, not the other candidate's rate. If the number can't work for both of us, I respect that." Calm, and it puts the decision back where it belongs.

The promotion variant

Internal asks change in one way: your value is documented and known, so the research argument matters less than the record. Bring the wins list — the projects, the scope you took on, the coverage during vacancies — and anchor to the market rate for the new role, not a percentage off your current one. The conversation opens: "I'd like to talk about the [new role] and the compensation that comes with it. Here's the scope I've already been carrying..."

What do you say when negotiating salary?

One sentence carries the ask: "Based on my research for this role and what I bring — [your strongest wins] — I was expecting a base salary closer to [number]. Is there flexibility?" Everything before it builds goodwill; everything after it is silence.

How do you negotiate salary over email?

Reply in writing with four parts: genuine enthusiasm for the role, the ask with one researched range, one supporting win, and thanks. Keep it under 150 words — the email's job is to book the conversation, not to have it.

What if they say the salary is not negotiable?

Accept the frame and switch levers: signing bonus, an earlier salary review with written criteria, extra leave, remote days, a title adjustment. If nothing moves, you've learned how the company treats its word before you've joined it — that is also useful information.

Should you accept the first job offer?

Ask once, politely — that's the whole rule. In ordinary experience, one courteous negotiation rarely costs an offer; employers expect it. What costs offers is how the asking is done: demanding, ultimatums, or negotiating after you've accepted. Ask once, kindly, then decide with the answer you get.
